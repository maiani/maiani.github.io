A magnetic twist that steers light

Put on a pair of polarized sunglasses, look at your phone, and slowly turn it. On most phones, at one angle the screen goes dark. The screen sends out polarized light: its electric field oscillates along a single direction. The lenses absorb light polarized along one direction and let the perpendicular one through, so when the screen’s direction matches the one the lenses absorb, nothing gets through. That direction is fixed once and for all when the lenses are made.

Probing the quantum energy landscape of a superconducting qubit Superconducting qubits rely on Josephson junctions—tiny nonlinear elements whose properties ultimately shape how well a qubit behaves. In theory these junctions create a simple cosine-shaped potential. In practice, real devices often carry small imperfections, such as higher harmonics in the potential, that subtly change the qubit’s energy levels and dynamics.
